Beat 1

The wall of brake lights

You're on the freeway at full speed. Up ahead, traffic is crawling. At first the slowdown is gradual: cars ease off a little, then a little more. But as more cars arrive from behind, that gentle slowdown gets squeezed into a sudden wall of brake lights.

Traffic, treated like a fluid

Since the 1950s, traffic engineers have described traffic not car by car, but like a fluid flowing down the road. That math predicts exactly what you just saw: a smooth slowdown that sharpens into a wall. It is the same equation we use on every page of this beat.

So here is our question. Suppose an AI learns only the rules of traffic, with no examples of real jams. Can it predict where that wall forms, and how sharp it gets? The kind of AI we test is a PINN: a neural network trained to obey the equation itself.

When drivers ease off early, the AI is excellent

If drivers start slowing down well ahead of the jam, the slowdown stays gentle. Here the PINN is genuinely impressive. After about 20 minutes of training, it draws the whole picture instantly.

0.05%how far the PINN's answer is from the exact one, when the slowdown is gentle

When drivers brake at the last second, it breaks

Now the wall gets sharp, and the PINN is off by about 40%. We tried giving it extra practice right at the wall. That helps when the wall is only moderately sharp, but at the sharpest wall it was still off by more than 40%.

~40%how far off the PINN is when the wall is sharp, with or without extra practice at the wall

Worse, the AI couldn't tell

While it trains, a PINN grades itself on how well it follows the rules. In one of our runs that grade improved more than 200 times, yet the answer stayed 35% wrong. On a real road there is no answer key to compare against, so that grade is all you would have.

200× / 35%the PINN's own score got 200 times better; its answer was still 35% wrong

Meanwhile, the traditional method

The kind of method engineers have used for decades, which steps forward in time across a grid, gets the sharp wall almost exactly, in a blink and with no training.

0.2% in 0.06 sthe traditional method on the sharpest wall, running in your browser

What this tells us

This AI is not useless: on smooth problems it is fast and accurate. But sharp changes trip it up, and its own score can't warn you when that happens. Someone still has to check its answer against something outside the AI. Keep that in mind; it comes back later.

The math, for the curious

The traffic model is the Lighthill–Whitham–Richards model (1955–56): road density ρ obeys ρt + (ρ(1 − ρ))x = 0 when car speed falls linearly from full speed (ρ = 0) to a standstill (ρ = 1). Writing u = 1 − 2ρ turns it into Burgers' equation, ut + u ux = 0. Car speed is then (1 + u)/2, which is what the chart above plots.

Our runs add a small smoothing term ν uxx. You can think of it as how far ahead drivers look; that reading is our analogy, not part of the basic traffic model. "Ease off early", "normal" and "last second" are ν = 0.01/π, 0.003/π and 0.001/π. The starting state is u = −sin(πx) on a road that wraps around, so the cars leaving on the right come back on the left. The chart is the exact solution (Cole–Hopf); the cars move at the local speed and are an illustration.

Numbers: 0.05% is the relative L2 error of our live model at ν = 0.01/π; about 40% is at ν = 0.001/π (median of three training seeds, uniform or shock-focused training points); 200× and 35% are one training run at ν = 0.003/π; 0.2% and 0.06 s are the finite-volume solver at ν = 0.001/π.

Source: LeVeque et al., Riemann Problems and Jupyter Solutions: Traffic flow.
