Does a good score mean a right answer?

A PINN learns by pushing one number down. This page shows that the number can look great while the answer is badly wrong. The number it trains on can't tell the difference, but a second check, which needs no exact answer, can.

1Two numbers, and a second check

While a PINN trains, it grades itself with one number, called the loss or score: how badly its answer breaks the equation at a fixed set of 10,000 check points. Lower is better, and training is just pushing this number down.

Training runs in two phases. Adam takes cautious steps to get roughly right; at step 2,000 we switch to L-BFGS, which uses the curvature of the score to home in precisely. This is the standard recipe for PINNs.

What we actually care about is the error: how far its answer is from the exact one. The PINN never sees the exact answer, so it cannot measure its own error. We can, because for this fluid problem the exact answer is known.

Think of checking a map only at the towns you visited. It can be perfect at every town and still draw the roads between them wrong. The way to catch that is to check it at towns you didn't visit. For a PINN, that means computing the same score on new points it never trained on. This needs no exact answer. It is the same idea as grading a model on data it hasn't seen (a "validation set") in data science.

3Why this happens

The score only checks the equation at its 10,000 check points, and those points never move. In the trap runs, the PINN puts the jump in the wrong place. By the end (time t = 1) its jump sits at x ≈ 0.1 to 0.2, while the real jump stays at x = 0. A misplaced jump breaks the equation badly, but only in a thin strip. Training, and L-BFGS above all, then bends the answer to fit the check points around that strip, so the score keeps falling while the strip stays wrong: the map that is right at every town and wrong between them. New points do land in the strip, which is why the same PINN scores thousands of times worse on them.

Putting extra check points at the jump closes the gap: the second button reaches about 1% error, and its score on new points is about the same as on its own. But we could only do that because we already knew the jump forms at x = 0. In a real problem you don't know where the trouble is.

The general fix needs no such knowledge. Every so often, throw the check points away and draw new ones, putting more of them wherever the equation is broken worst right now (Wu and others, 2023). Nobody tells the PINN where the jump is: the broken equation points there by itself. Click Same fluid, points that move to the trouble. It starts from the same network and the same check points as the trap, and ends 0.8% off instead of 35%, with the jump in the right place. All three runs like it ended 0.7% to 1.4% off, as good as the runs that were told where the jump is, and at the end their score on new points was no worse than on their own points. Aiming matters: when we redrew the points evenly instead, without aiming them at the trouble, the PINN often overfit each new set within its 1,000 steps, and the three runs ended 0.4%, 50% and 69% off. It is not a cure-all, though: in the thin fluid, where the jump is sharpest, our one run with moving points still ended about 50% off, no better than the others there. We did not try stronger settings (packing the points harder, or redrawing them more often).

Is L-BFGS to blame? As a check, we trained the trap's three networks with Adam alone for 50,000 steps, about four times the work the trap's L-BFGS phase did. Click Control: more Adam, no L-BFGS. Adam never got the answer right: after step 10,000 its error kept jumping between about 14% and more than 100%, and it ended 45% to 85% off. And whenever its score dipped low, the gap between its score on its own points and on new points opened up too, to as much as about 200 times. So L-BFGS is not the cause: with enough training, fixed check points get overfit. L-BFGS pushes the score lowest, so its gap grows to thousands.

4Key takeaways

  • The score measures how well the PINN obeys the equation at its own check points, not how close it is to the truth. Most of the time the two agree.
  • In the medium fluid with evenly spread points they came apart. In the trap run, the score improved about 20× in its last ~4,000 steps while the error only went from 38% to 35%. In the two other runs like it, the score fell 12× and 25× after the switch while the error got worse. On new points, all three scored thousands of times worse than on their own.
  • In the thin fluid the score stayed high, above every run that got the answer right and mostly by hundreds of times, so there it honestly flagged the failure. The score only misled us in the medium case.
  • Extra check points at the jump fixed the error, but only because we knew where the jump would form. Check points that keep moving to where the equation breaks fixed it just as well (0.7% to 1.4% error) without that knowledge. Redrawing them evenly, without aiming, was hit or miss (0.4%, 50% and 69%). In the thin fluid, neither fix worked.
  • It is not just L-BFGS. Adam alone, given about four times the work, never got the answer right (45% to 85% off at the end), and whenever its score dipped, the same gap opened (up to about 200 times). With enough training, fixed check points get overfit; L-BFGS just pushes hardest.
  • The PINN weakness: the score a PINN trains on is a training score, and like any training score it can overfit the points it is graded on. The PINN can't notice that by itself. Grading it on points it never trained on catches the trap and needs no answer key, but a person has to think to ask for it.
The math, for the curious

The score (loss) is \[L = 0.1\,\overline{r^2} + 0.1\,L_{\text{IC}} + 0.1\,L_{\text{BC}}, \qquad r = u_t + u\,u_x - \nu\, u_{xx},\] where \(\overline{r^2}\) is the mean squared residual of Burgers' equation over the 10,000 collocation points (the check points), and \(L_{\text{IC}}\), \(L_{\text{BC}}\) are the mean squared misfits of the starting state and of the two edges, \(x = -1\) and \(x = 1\). The score on new points is the same \(L\), with \(\overline{r^2}\) averaged over 200,000 new points drawn uniformly from \(x \in [-1, 1]\), \(t \in [0, 1]\) instead; it is the same set for every run, and the start and end terms are unchanged. Because the score averages a squared residual, a 100× drop in the score means the residual itself shrank only about 10×.

The error is the relative \(L^2\) error against the Cole–Hopf exact solution over a 256 × 100 grid in \((x, t)\). Training runs Adam (learning rate \(10^{-3}\)) for 2,000 steps, then L-BFGS for up to 15,000 steps, stopping after 1,000 steps without improvement. "Before the switch" in the readout is the median over Adam steps 1,500–2,000, because single Adam steps jump around. Thick, medium and thin fluid are \(\nu = 0.01/\pi\), \(0.003/\pi\) and \(0.001/\pi\). "Extra check points at the jump" draws half of the 10,000 points with \(x \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\, 0.05^2)\), which uses our prior knowledge that the jump forms at \(x = 0\). The jump position quoted above is where the PINN's \(u(x, 1)\) falls fastest.

"Points that move to the trouble" is residual-based adaptive sampling (RAD) with \(k = c = 1\). Every 250 Adam steps, and at the start of each of 15 rounds of 1,000 L-BFGS steps, the 10,000 check points are redrawn from 100,000 uniform candidates; each candidate is picked with probability proportional to \(|r| / \overline{|r|} + 1\), so about half of the points go where the residual is large and half stay spread out. Each round starts a new L-BFGS, because L-BFGS remembers the curvature of the old score, which changes when the points do. Moving points are not a perfect guard. In the three medium-fluid runs, within the first L-BFGS round, before the first redraw, the PDE term on new points still pulled 8 to 23 times ahead of the one on its own points; after that it stayed between 0.5 and 3.4 times, and it ended at about 0.6 (below 1, because the PINN's own points sit where the equation is hardest). In the thin-fluid run it ended about 2,000 times ahead: each round was long enough to overfit the new points. "Evenly redrawn" uses the same schedule with uniform points.

The general fix that needs no such knowledge is residual-based adaptive sampling: Wu, Zhu, Tan, Kartha & Lu, "A comprehensive study of non-adaptive and residual-based adaptive sampling for physics-informed neural networks", Comput. Methods Appl. Mech. Engrg. 403 (2023) 115671, arXiv:2207.10289.
