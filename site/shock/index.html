How a smooth wave turns into a cliff

1The question

We start with a smooth wave of moving fluid. Fluid on the left side moves right, fluid on the right side moves left, so everything heads toward the middle. Each blue line in the top picture follows one bit of fluid as it moves at its starting speed.

2Try it

Press Play, or Jump to the first crossing. Then set the thickness to thin and watch the jump in the bottom picture become an almost vertical wall.
paths of fluid bits zero-thickness fluid (ideal) fluid at the chosen thickness if the bits passed through each other (impossible)

3Why it happens

The faster bits catch up with the slower ones ahead of them. At time \(t = 1/\pi \approx 0.318\) the lines first cross at the middle (\(x = 0\)), and the smooth wave becomes a sudden jump, called a shock.

The bottom picture shows the fluid's speed at each position. The thickness menu sets how thick (viscous) the fluid is: thick fluid rounds the jump off a little, thin fluid makes it almost a vertical wall.

4Key takeaways

  • Faster fluid catches up with slower fluid; where the paths cross, a sudden jump (a shock) forms, first at \(t = 1/\pi \approx 0.318\).
  • Thickness (viscosity) rounds the jump off. The thinner the fluid, the closer it gets to a vertical wall.
  • Sharp jumps like this are exactly what trips up the PINN on the next pages.
