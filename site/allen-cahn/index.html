The step-by-step simulator

This is how a traditional simulation works, and the one thing it can never do.

1A mixture that separates

Picture a thin rod filled with a mixture of two materials, like oil and water. The curve shows the mix along the rod: near +1 is mostly one material, near −1 mostly the other, and 0 is half and half. Over time the materials separate and the curve settles into a resting state, a shape that no longer changes.

2The rule: always roll downhill

This simulator works the traditional way: it moves forward in time with many small steps. Each step can only lower the energy, like a ball that can only roll downhill. So it settles into stable resting states, the bowls, and can never stay on an unstable one, a hilltop.

3Try it

Press Play with random noise: it always settles into one hump. Then pick S-shape (a hilltop) and press Play. It balances for a while, then the computer's own rounding errors (about 0.0000000000000001) tip it off, a little after time 20. Pick S-shape + tiny nudge at time 8 and it falls sooner.

The mix along the rod: now (blue), at the start (gray)

from any random start, the blue curve ends as one hump (or the same hump upside down). The S-shape only lasts while it is perfectly balanced.

Energy over time: it can only go down, as long as the time step is small enough (values above 1 are cut off)

the orange line never goes up. On the S-shape it sits flat at −0.35, then drops to the one-hump level, −2.40, once the curve rolls off the hilltop.

Known answers to check against: the one-hump resting state has energy −2.4036 and height 0.9825; the S-shape has energy −0.3541 and height 0.6601. The menu also lists the five exact resting states and the PINN's guesses.

4Key takeaways

  • A step-by-step simulation can only go downhill in energy, so it always ends in a stable resting state.
  • That makes it reliable: from almost any start it finds the bowls on its own, with no training.
  • Its blind spot: it can never stay on a hilltop state like the S-shape. Even rounding errors tip it off.
  • Where PINNs help: a PINN aims at the resting shape directly instead of stepping forward in time, so it can land on a hilltop. Here it is not just a worse solver; it does a job the simulation cannot.
The math, for the curious

Allen–Cahn equation ut = uxx + μ(u − u³) on (0, π), u = 0 at both ends, μ = 6, N = 128 grid cells, forward Euler time steps (stable for Δt ≤ h²/2). Forward Euler here is gradient descent on the discrete energy Eh, which is why the energy can only go down.
