Does a good score mean a right answer?

A PINN learns by pushing one number down. This page shows that the number can look great while the answer is badly wrong, and that the PINN has no way to tell the difference.

1Two numbers: the score and the truth

While a PINN trains, it grades itself with one number, called the loss: how badly its answer breaks the equation at a set of check points. Lower is better, and training is just pushing this number down.

What we actually care about is the error: how far its answer is from the exact one. The PINN never sees the exact answer, so it cannot measure its own error. We can, because for this fluid problem the exact answer is known.

Think of checking a map only at the towns you visited. It can be perfect at every town and still draw the roads between them wrong.

3Why this happens

The score only checks the equation at the check points. Near the jump the fluid's speed changes very quickly, so a PINN can obey the equation well at every point it checks and still get the jump wrong in between, like the map that is right at every town. Putting extra check points at the jump closes that gap, which is why the second button reaches about 1% error at a similar score.

4Key takeaways

  • The score (loss) measures how well the PINN obeys the equation at its check points, not how close it is to the truth.
  • Near a sharp jump the two can disagree completely: the score improved more than 200× while the answer stayed 35% off.
  • In this case, moving the check points to the jump fixed the error. More training did not.
  • The PINN weakness: in a real problem nobody has the exact answer, so the score is the only number you get, and it cannot tell you whether you landed in the trap or got a good run.
The math, for the curious

The loss is the mean squared residual of Burgers' equation ut + u ux − ν uxx = 0 at the collocation points, plus the initial-condition and boundary terms. The error is the relative L² error against the Cole–Hopf exact solution over the whole x–t grid. Training runs Adam, then switches to L-BFGS at step 2000. Thick, medium and thin fluid are ν = 0.01/π, 0.003/π and 0.001/π; "extra check points at the jump" means collocation points concentrated near the shock at x = 0.
