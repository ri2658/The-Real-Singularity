Where a PINN breaks

Burgers' equation ut + u ux = ν uxx starts from a smooth sine wave and steepens into a shock at x = 0. The smaller the viscosity ν, the sharper the shock. Our PINN, trained the same way each time, nails the smooth case and falls apart as the shock sharpens. Slide ν and t to see where.

Why the shock is hard. A PINN is a smooth function (tanh layers) trained only to make the equation's residual small at sample points. At ν = 0.001/π the solution drops by about 1.5 over a width of a less than a thousandth, narrower than the spacing of the grid we compare on. The network can't bend that sharply without huge derivatives. A shock that is smeared, or sits slightly in the wrong place, still makes the residual small almost everywhere, so training settles on the wrong answer: in the uniform run, the PINN's jump at t = 1 sits a few hundredths to the right of the true one.

Why more points at the shock isn't enough. Putting half the training points near x = 0 rescues ν = 0.003/π, from about 49% error down to about 1.3%, but not ν = 0.001/π: still about 45%. A finite-volume solver gets the same shock to about 0.1% error (see the race page).
