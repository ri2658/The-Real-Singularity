Falling off a hilltop

Can a simulation stay on a hilltop if you are careful enough? This page measures how long it holds on.

1The question

The S-shaped mixture from the simulator is a resting state, but an unstable one, like a pencil balanced on its tip. Nudge it by a tiny amount and it eventually tips over into the stable one-hump shape.

So how long does it hold on? If you make the nudge much smaller, does it stay up much longer?

2Try it

Press Run with the default nudge (one millionth) and watch the blue curve peel away from the gray S-shape. Then press Run every size to try ten nudges, from one hundredth down to one hundred-billionth.

3Why the same 1.5 every time?

Near the hilltop, a nudge grows by the same factor every moment, like money earning compound interest: it gets about 10 times bigger every 1.5 time units. A nudge 10 times smaller just needs 1.5 more time units to catch up. The simulator only takes small steps forward and never sees this rule, yet its points land on the line.

4Key takeaways

  • A smaller nudge only delays the fall: about 1.5 more time units for each 10× smaller nudge.
  • Even a nudge of one hundred-billionth falls in about 15 time units, and the computer's own rounding errors are enough to start the fall.
  • So no amount of care keeps a step-by-step simulation on a hilltop. To study hilltop states you need a method that solves for the resting shape directly, such as Newton's method or a PINN.
  • What this means for PINNs: finding unstable states is a real job where a PINN is useful, not a worse copy of the simulator.
The math, for the curious

Near the S state of the Allen–Cahn equation, a push of size ε grows like ε·eλ₁t, where λ₁ ≈ 1.5294 is the one unstable growth rate of S. The curve counts as "escaped" once it is 0.1 away from S, so the escape time is about ln(0.1/ε)/λ₁. Making ε ten times smaller adds ln(10)/λ₁ ≈ 1.5056. The simulator is plain forward Euler.
