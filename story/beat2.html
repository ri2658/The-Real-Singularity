Beat 2

Balls in bowls, balls on hilltops

Some answers to an equation are stable, like a ball in a bowl. Others are unstable, like a ball on a hilltop: still, until the smallest nudge sends it rolling. Which tools can find each kind?

Why hilltops matter: how things change shape

Many things in nature work by switching between two stable shapes. Proteins, the cell's molecular machines, are the classic example. Every switch must climb an energy barrier, and the easiest route crosses exactly one unstable shape: a mountain pass called the transition state. Its height decides how often the switch happens.

Simulations struggle here: they spend almost all their time jiggling in one bowl, so catching a single switch can take months of computing. In 2025, researchers trained a physics-informed network to draw the path over the pass directly. For a protein of over 3,500 atoms, it found the same shape change in minutes on an ordinary graphics card, instead of weeks on a computing cluster.

To see why that pass is hard to find, we turn to something far simpler, where we know every answer: a rod of tiny magnets.

A rod of tiny magnets

Picture a thin rod lined with tiny magnets, like compass needles. Each wants to point fully up or down, and the same way as its neighbours; the two ends are held pointing neither way. Left alone, the rod settles into a resting shape that no longer changes. The equation behind this, Allen–Cahn, works like a landscape: every arrangement has an energy, a height, and the rod always moves downhill. Resting shapes are the flat spots.

\[\begin{gathered} u_t = u_{xx} + \mu\,(u - u^3), \\ x \in (0, \pi),\quad u(0, t) = u(\pi, t) = 0,\quad \mu = 6. \end{gathered}\]

Here \(u(x, t)\) is which way the magnet at position \(x\) points, from \(-1\) (down) to \(+1\) (up). The \(u_{xx}\) term makes neighbours agree; \(\mu\,(u - u^3)\) pushes each magnet to fully up or fully down. The landscape is the energy

\[E[u] = \int_0^{\pi} \Big(\tfrac12\,u_x^2 - \mu\big(\tfrac12\,u^2 - \tfrac14\,u^4\big)\Big)\,dx,\]

and a resting shape is any \(u\) with \(u_{xx} + \mu\,(u - u^3) = 0\), where the rod stops changing.

Our rod has exactly five, all computed precisely: a hump (the middle points up), an S-shape (left half up, right half down), each of these flipped, and every magnet stuck in between.

Only two survive. The humps sit in bowls: nudge one and it settles back. The S-shapes and the in-between state sit on hilltops: the smallest nudge sends them rolling into a hump. Since heat keeps every needle jiggling, you never see a hilltop shape for long.

To watch this, we use a step-by-step simulator built on the classic forward Euler method: it splits the rod into 128 small pieces and moves time forward in tiny steps, each one going downhill in energy. The exact resting shapes above come from a different classic solver, Newton's method, which solves directly for shapes that don't change.

The rod can switch like the protein: the two humps play its two shapes, and the lowest route between them crosses one pass, the S-shape.

A simulation can never stop on that pass, yet the pass decides how the rod switches. That is why a tool that can aim at hilltops, like a PINN, is useful, as long as something checks what it finds. The demos below show both.

So far

  • The simulator (forward Euler) finds all five resting shapes, and how strongly each pulls nearby shapes in or pushes them away, with no training.
  • But it only rolls downhill, so it can never stop on a hilltop.
  • A PINN can aim at a hilltop directly: told to look for an S-shape, it lands within about 6% of it.
  • PINN limit 1: it can't tell a bowl from a hilltop. Its score is just as good for both; only running its answer through the simulator shows the S falls off.
  • PINN limit 2: a near-perfect score can mean a useless answer, such as a flat, in-between shape that is neither S-shape nor hump.
  • So each tool does a different job, and a PINN's answer always needs an outside check.

Going deeper: PINNs and blow-ups

From a steep wall to a blow-up

For the curious: the same bowl-and-hilltop idea sits at the centre of one of the biggest open questions in mathematics.

In Beat 1, the later drivers braked, the steeper the wall of brake lights. The thinner the fluid, the closer that wall comes to vertical; with no thickness at all, its steepness becomes infinite. That is a blow-up, and it is where our PINN failed, about 40% off. No surprise: a PINN is built from smooth pieces, so it can never draw an infinite slope.

Why blow-ups matter

The Navier–Stokes equations of fluid flow underlie weather forecasts, aircraft design and blood-flow models. Yet nobody knows whether they always give a sensible answer: could a smooth flow of water or air spin itself up until its speed becomes infinite? If so, the equations are incomplete; if not, they never break down.

$1,000,000the prize for settling it either way: one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems

Two kinds of blow-up

Blow-ups come in two kinds. A stable one is a ball in a bowl: change the starting flow slightly and it still blows up the same way, so a simulation can find it. An unstable one is a ball on a hilltop: it needs a perfectly tuned start, and the tiniest error knocks a simulation off it.

Some mathematicians suspect any Navier–Stokes blow-up would be unstable. In 2025, mathematicians and Google DeepMind researchers trained physics-informed networks to find such fragile blow-ups directly, and found new ones in simpler fluid equations. That doesn't solve the million-dollar problem, but it shows what PINNs might be good for. Our rod's S-shape is the same story in miniature.
